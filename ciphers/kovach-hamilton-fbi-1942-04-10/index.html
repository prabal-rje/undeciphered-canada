The Hamilton wartime cipher J. Edgar Hoover's FBI lab refused to crack

Paul Kovach (244 James St. N., Hamilton, ON) to FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover · 10 April 1942

Historical context

On 10 April 1942, Paul Kovach of 244 James Street North, Hamilton, Ontario (address of the Niagara Lunch in 1942 Hamilton directories) mailed a two-page handwritten numeric cipher letter directly to FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover. Stamped 'RECEIVED APR 13 1942 TECHNICAL LABORATORY F.B.I.', the cipher went unread: in his 30 April 1942 reply (File 80-612-73, frame n218), Hoover declined to analyze it, citing 'the great amount of current investigations presently before the Bureau and the limited amount of personnel in its Technical Laboratory.' Cryptanalysis recovers Kovach's entire structured two-digit substitution table (grouped in descending/ascending alphabet triples: A/B/C=99/95/93, D/E/F=86/85/82, G/H/X=13/15/17, K/L/M=24/26/28, N/O/P=75/77/79, R/S/T=61/58/52, U/V/W=31/33/35, Y=44), his homophonic hundreds series for 'I' (100/200/300/900), his fraction doublers (1/2 and 1/3 repeating the previous letter in ALL, I'LL, APPRECIATE, SOON, HOOVER, GOOD), and single-digit word shorthands (6=IF, 8=TO). The decoded letter turns out to be an earnest, humorous wartime job application from an immigrant Canadian asking Hoover to train him as a detective, quipped as teaching him 'the American Fox Trot and the Two Step.'

Decoded message (plain text)

Dear Sir. On doubt I write. you the few line to ask you[0]. If [0]you could give me any position, probably, to learn for detective All you got to do Mr. Hoover to teach me for the American Fox Trot and the Two Step [9]. If you have [2]. won't [obscured]n me, but no chance. I'll [4] appreciate if I can hear from you soon [1:as?] you read them. If you please. Good bye. Yours very truly, Paul Kovach

How it was decoded & archive details

  • How it was readCracked straight from the scrambled message without a codebook or key on file.
  • Discovery statusUnread until now: recovered from original archive code with no earlier published translation found.
  • Code & keyCustom numeric monoalphabetic substitution (23/26 letters in triples) + homophonic I (100/200/300/900) + fraction doublers (1/2, 1/3) + single-digit word shorthands (6=IF, 8=TO)
  • Archive referenceFBI Headquarters File 80-612-73 (Cryptography 080-HQ-612), frames n218-n220 (pp. 219-221)
  • VerificationHoover's 30 April 1942 reply on the immediately preceding frame (n218) explicitly confirms the FBI Technical Laboratory did not analyze the cipher, and no plaintext or key appears anywhere in the 356-page file or online. Mechanical decryption of all 71 word-groups yields coherent English across 23 distinct letters (224/225 letter/doubler tokens exact) with consistent arithmetic triple structure and cross-checked shorthands (6=IF 4/4, 8=TO 3/3).
  • Notes & prior researchOne word at top of page 2 (frame n220, line 1: '[hole]5752885,' = '[obscured]n me,') is partially obliterated by an archival binder punch hole. Five single-occurrence single-digit tokens (0, 1, 2, 4, 9) are retained transparently in brackets rather than guessed (context strongly favors 1='as' in 'soon [as] you read them', 2='it'/'time', 4='be'/'so', 9='too', and 0 as a separator/null on adjacent 'you'). Kovach made three adjacent-key slips (93 for 95 in DAUCT='doubt', 75 for 77 in FNR='for', 95 for 93 in BHONCE='chance'), dropped one digit in 937731[2]86='could', and used phonetic spellings ('prabably', 'lean', 'detecive', 'Hooer', 'Amarican', 'appresiate', 'biy').
  • Archival sourceFederal Bureau of Investigation, Headquarters File 80-612-73 (Cryptography 080-HQ-612), scan frames n218-n220, via Internet Archive (nsia-fbi-files).
  • RightsDeclassified U.S. federal archive record (FBI FOIA release 80-HQ-612) containing personal correspondence submitted to a public agency in 1942.
